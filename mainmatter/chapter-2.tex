Three consequences follow. First, no deployment-accuracy requirement is defined, so the thesis derives its own target rather than inheriting one, which is why the accuracy budget is a deliverable in its own right (\Cref{sec:phase2}). Its inputs and assumptions are stated alongside the outputs of the analyses, so that the budget can be updated and fed back to the design report as the instrument design settles.

Second, the separation does not sit on one side of the boundary between the two architectural regimes, it spans it. Those regimes are the folding architectures, whose arms have to lie against the bus, and the extending architectures, which do not carry that bound; they are set out in \Cref{sec:families-assessed}. With a \SI{30}{\centi\meter} primary, a separation of about \SI{30}{\centi\meter} is roughly the primary diameter and therefore at the limit of the folding architectures, while the \SI{60}{\centi\meter} bound is about twice it and beyond them.

Third, the upper bound comes from the platform rather than from the optics: a long instrument presents a large baffle area in the velocity direction, so drag sets a limit on length, and the design report names the thermo-mechanical stability of a deployable telescope alongside it. The separation is therefore not a free variable in the parametric sweep: it is swept only up to that platform-imposed bound, which is carried as a constraint on the model.
